Chairman’s Corner

eam - In this edition we dedicate our magazine to those communicators that supported our special operations community. These incredible young men and women have often led the technological developments for our Army through the unique requirements to ensure their special operators could rapidly move, shoot, communicate and strike anywhere in the world. The stories of these accomplishments begin to end some of the mystique that surrounds our special operations community and will give you some great insights into the brave men and women who solved the wicked problem sets that truly enabled these special team of warriors. I hope you will enjoy reading the stories, seeing the pictures and reliving just some of the history that this edition will bring to light. I challenge you to join us in inspiring the next generation to service above self as we unlock and share the history of accomplishments that have occurred throughout our signal and cyber mission forces. Many thanks to our General Officers, Senior Executives and Sergeants Major who, along with each of you, continue to support the Historical Museum Society. You can join our team by making a donation, contributing a story, picture or volunteering to help us share these stories. In that spirit I am including the picture below that was taken back in 1994 when I was a Company Commander of an Airborne Signal Company at Fort Bragg that supported the XVIII Airborne Corps Main Command Post. As we were preparing to conduct an airborne operation on green ramp I got a call that the Corps Commander would be joining us for this mission. Upon meeting General Shelton, he asked about our operation and immediately put me at ease with his confident calm demeanor. General Shelton is a legend and having come out of our special operations community I was nervous that our preparation would not meet his high standards. As I briefed him on our airborne assault and follow-on mission, I could see that he was pleased with our planning, attention to detail and scope of the mission. I was amazed that this senior leader would take the time to come down inspect our training and to jump with us. His leadership by example is something I would never forget. General Shelton would go on to become the Chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff and I never forgot his leadership example and I was honored to support and serve alongside him at Fort Bragg. His outstanding example of leadership is emblematic of how our special operations community “lead from the front.” Many thanks to all of our special operator communicators and leaders for all you do as you truly are amongst our most elite warriors.

Q&A with Brigadier General Jeth Rey

1. Tell us about your upbringing. Where were you born and raised and what was it like for you?

I was born on a small island of Anguilla, in the British West Indies. At three months, my mother moved to Saint

Thomas, U.S. Virgin Islands. We were pretty poor: it was not extravagant. My mom was a single parent. I was youngest of eight kids. My mother taught me a lot growing up that helped me throughout my years. She really trusted me as a young kid of seven or eight years old. She used to let me go to the grocery store and go to the bank to cash her check. So I learned responsibility and financial responsibility. It taught me discipline, responsibility, organization, maturity, and focus: traits I have carried with me throughout my like.

My first time in the US was at 13 yrs old when I moved to Virginia to visit my sister. From there, I attended junior high school and high school. I transferred because I wanted to get into an environment that was a little bit more conducive to learning.

2. What prompted you to join the army?

In high school, I joined the Navy JROTC. Truly, the main reason I joined was because I needed extra clothes to wear to school and more importantly a jacket to stay warm. They issued us pea coats with our uniform and I needed a coat to keep me warm because typically I walked to school. I prospered in JROTC and eventually I made it from a young cadet to the commander of our program.

I joined the Army right out of high school at 17 yrs old. We couldn’t afford college and plus my grades weren’t as great as they should’ve been in order for me to get a scholarship. Since I thrived in Navy JROTC, I went to the Navy recruiter and wanted to leave that day but they couldn’t get me out of the door quickly enough. I went to the USMC recruiter and again they really couldn’t get me out the door quick enough. I went to the Army and the Army was ready to go. So the next morning I left for Basic training at Ft. Dix, New Jersey.

3. Why Signal Regiment?

My recruiter told me that Signal would be a great choice. In 1983, Signal was more analog so I became a signaleer within the analog environment. My first duty assignment was in Korea. I was a tactical signal operator, laying wire, retrans systems, and operating the radio. My platoon Sergeant encouraged me to become an Airborne Soldier; so I submitted my 4187 and was selected to attend Airborne school. Subsequently, I was assigned to the 4th Psychological Operations Group (SO)(A). This was my introduction into Special Operations in 1985. I would spend the next 24 years assigned to numerous Special Operations units.

SCAMPI

by John R. Thomas, Army Colonel (Retired)

The US Special Operations Command was established in 1987 under Major Force Program 11 (MFP 11). The purpose was to consolidate all Special Operations Forces (SOF) under one command for funding, training, acquisition, TTP and Command and Control Direct Action when directed.

Prior to this consolidation the communications systems of the three service SOF organizations and the Joint Special Operations Command (JSOC) were stovepiped and non-interoperable. They lacked the capacity to move critical data and imagery to SOF units when needed for planning, targeting and execution. Furthermore the acquisition and operations and maintenance were enormous. Of particular need was to provide quality imagery to JSOC. The legacy JSOC system relied on many point to point 56 KB circuits. Compounding the data movement deficiency was the lack of a common TS and TS(SCI) switched voice system to effect C2.

SCAMPI

Scalable Copy Accelerated by MPI (SCAMPI) is a command line utility to accelerate data transfer, and is currently installed on most HPCMP HPC systems.

Page 11

One may think that the SCAMPI system was a darling of imagination, however, this would be misleading. A request from JSOC came into USSOCOM for additional funding for more 56 KB circuits to enhance their image distribution system. Back of the envelope calculations showed that for the price of three 56 KB circuits a full T1 circuit could be acquired.This provided a 24 to 1 increase in capacity over a single 56 KB circuit at one eighth to cost. Remember this was 1987 and the use of T1 was new to theDoD.

The finances were a no brainer. The challenge was how to maximize the bandwidth for other critical services, e.g. unclassified voice, TS voice and TS(SCI) voice Fax and etc. (This was before the Internet TCP/IP and WWW technology were widespread). To solve this the SCAMPI architecture was designed to “tunnel” through a TS (SCI) high system using super encrypted channels on the T1 to separate the unclassified voice and TS Red Switch trunks. This was a novel approach to say the least.

SCAMPI originally started as a hub and spoke network but quickly evolved to a highly survivable mesh. In 1990 deployable SCAMPI nodes were implemented. Soon after TCP/IP and web based applications became available.

The success of SCAMPI brought attention from other elements of the DoD and especially DISA. Although the TS(SCI) system high architecture was not copied it did provide the primary impetus for the Defense Information Systems Network (DISN). DISA later unsuccessfully tried to roll SCAMPI into the DISN.Eventual integration and cooperation ensued.

The rapid implementation of SCAMPI was the product of the general SOF approach to meeting any mission. Streamlined acquisition, bottom up requirement development and agility were prime factors. Decision authority was pushed down to appropriate levels. Funding decisions were quick.Implementation and operations teams were small.

Their small signal element, consisting of two, three-man Special Operations Communications Assemblage (SOCA) teams, was providing secure communications for the Special Operations Command, South (SOCSOUTH), led by Colonel (COL) Robert C. ‘Jake’ Jacobelly.

Later, while he scanned his target from a cow pasture on the west side of the Pacora River, MAJ Higgins talked with Lieutenant Colonel (LTC) David J. Cleveland (S-3, 3-7th SFG) “like they were standing next to me.” The SOCA team at Albrook provided Wilderman and Cleveland the situational awareness to prioritize highdemand assets, like the AC -130 Spectre gunship and the quick reaction force (QRF) at Albrook. Once committed, SOCSOUTH had nothing to reinforce other elements in contact. Clear and timely communications were essential to mission success on the opening night of Operation JUST CAUSE, and the 112th Signal Battalion SOCAs delivered. They continued to do so throughout the operation.

This article details 112th Signal Battalion origins and explains how it was manned and equipped as a dedicated special operations communications battalion. The battalion’s ‘trial-by-fire’ in Panama, 1989 – 1990 is highlighted. The focus is the SOCA teams that provided SOCSOUTH and ARSOF elements secure tactical communications during Operation JUST CAUSE and the follow-on stability operation, PROMOTE LIBERTY.

About the same time, forwardthinking officers began ‘beating the drum’ for a signal unit capable of supporting both 1st SOCOM and theaterlevel Joint Unconventional Warfare Task Forces (JUWTF).

Based at Patch Barracks, Stuttgart (Vaihingen), Germany, the SSD's mission was to "provide initial wartime communications support to Commander, SOCEUR (COMSOCEUR) and US Special Operations liaison elements at various NATO HQ." Additionally, SSD was tasked to "provide C2 communications support to COMSOCEUR in support of contingency and special operations. A third task was to serve as the personal communications support for the Deputy Commander, US European Command. (3)

Almost immediately, SSD was tested in real-world missions. Their first operational mission supported the 1986 Operation ELDORADO CANYON (the US response to Libya based terrorist attacks including a Berlin nightclub bombing). Other early missions included support to hostage rescue operations (1986 to 1991), Operation STEEL BOX (removal of chemical weapons from Europe), and deployments to Turkey for Operations DESERT STORM, and PROVIDE COMFORT, both in 1991. In this latter mission, SSD earned the Joint Meritorious Unit Award. (4)

This unique support was only possible because of the close coordination between SSD and the SOCEUR J6 Directorate. While SSD provided the manpower and operators, the SOCEUR J6 spearheaded the innovation and secured the resources needed. Understanding the requirements of the SOCEUR deployable HQs is where SOCEUR J6 developed the ideas and systems that SSD would train to proficiently operate. These requirements served to fuel creative application of technology, training methods, and deployment techniques key to both their past successes and opportunities for the future.

Going into the 1990s, SSD often deployed, focused on three types of missions: rapid deployment to the continent of Africa for a variety of non-combatant

evacuation operations (NEO), humanitarian assistance, and low-intensity combat/counterterrorism. Several of these missions required small teams to launch on short notice as part of the European Survey and Assessment Team (ESAT) providing initial connectivity between USEUCOM and the crisis area. Additionally, when required, a larger contingent of the Detachment deployed to provide C2 communications for HQ SOCEUR when it formed a Joint Special Operations Task Force (JSOTF).

SSD was also part of the various peace keeping and stabilization efforts in Bosnia-Herzegovina beginning in 1996 (Operations JOINT FORGE, JOINT GUARDIAN, ALLIED FORCE, FERVENT ARCHER).

In 1997, then Lt Colonel William "Bronco" Lane published a paper as part of his Naval War College studies, considering the tenets for successful communications supporting special operations. (5) He previously commanded the 112th Special Operations Signal Battalion (Airborne) at Fort Bragg. Within this unit were several 15-Soldier Signal Detachments, including one earmarked for Germany. Part of his thesis was that the mission for SOCEUR support should belong to the 112th, and that such support represented

"mission creep" for the mission of SSD's current parent, 52d Signal Battalion.

The paper touched off a series of discussions and analyses to determine the best way to provide communications support to SOCEUR, and all the various Theater Special Operations commands. He suggested that the 42-Soldier SOCEUR detachment was not sustainable in each Theater, while the smaller 15-Soldier variant limited operational flexibility.

In May of 2000, US Special Operations Command and the US Army agreed to re-form the SOCEUR Signal Detachment as a 32-Soldier unit, assigned to 112th Special Operations Signal Battalion. In so doing, the 15-Soldier "Germany" detachment merged with 17 spaces from the existing 52d Signal Battalion SSD. The goal was to complete the transfer and reorganization by 30 September 2002.

On 2 April 2002, Captain Brett Patron and First Sergeant Allan Fairley cased the guidon of SOCEUR Signal Detachment (Airborne), 52d Signal Battalion, presenting it to Lt Colonel Cyrus Gywn. They then received the new guidon of Signal Detachment (Europe), 112th Special Operations Signal Battalion (Airborne) from Lt Colonel Robert Bell. In attendance at the ceremony on Patch Barracks was Colonel "Bronco" Lane, commanding 2d Signal Brigade, the 52d Signal Battalion's higher headquarters.

From the beginning, battalion leaders promoted a competitive mindset. The soldiers responded, believing that theirs was a special unit, due to its unique ARSOF support mission. Team sports and battalion runs built unit cohesion. Physical training was tough. Expectations were high, initiative was rewarded, and leaders were held accountable. Armed with Army studies and requirements documents, such as the Army Mission Area Analysis (1983), Special Operations Forces Master Plan (1984), and the Multi-command Required Operational Capability 2-84 (1984), Bryan prepared to brief his plan to the Army. He recognized that the Signal Corps would be reluctant to support the creation of a dedicated “ARSOF Communications Support Element,” which borrowed from Joint terminology; a signal battalion similar to those in the ‘Big Army’ would be an easier sell. Bryan identified three specific needs for an ARSOF communications battalion: a flexible structure that allowed SOF communications packages to be tailored to the supported mission; a modernization strategy that ensured ARSOF communicators had state-of-the-art equipment, through priority placement on the Department of the Army Master Priority List; and a “professional home for ARSOF communicators” that grounded them for continuity and did not stunt their career progression. His pitch succeeded, and the Army approved Table of Organization and Equipment (TO&E) 11015J500, “Special Operations Communications Battalion,” on 1 April 1985.

Bryan also understood that most conventional Army communications systems were too bulky for ARSOF’s short notice, small footprint deployments. To get the right equipment, the battalion customized standard Army signal systems to meet mission requirements

Conclusion

The 112th Signal Battalion presence in Panama peaked at 30 personnel in early January 1990. It included seven SOCA teams, LTC Sawdey, CSM Black, and several staff planners. Some 112th soldiers redeployed back to Fort Bragg in January and, by the end of March 1990, only a small element, led by CPT Eric G. David, was supporting stability operations.

Although limited in numbers, the ‘Shadow Warriors’ in Panama ‘punched above their weight,’ providing SOCSOUTH and other ARSOF units secure communications when theater systems were overwhelmed. MAJ Kropp reported that, “in many cases, the 112th provided the only communications lifeline available to deployed Special Operations elements” in Panama. LTC Sawdey described the performance of his veterans as “magnificent.”

Some of those who spent Christmas 1989 in Panama would spend the next one in Saudi Arabia supporting Operation DESERT SHIELD. LTC Sawdey was not among them. He received orders for the Army War College, and handed over command to LTC Samuel R. Higdon in June 1990. Prior to leaving, Sawdey addressed his battalion. Noting the lack of fanfare for ARSOF returning from Panama, he told his soldiers, “It’s in the nature of our character to be quiet professionals. Our identity is to be defined by what we accomplish, not who we are.” Every soldier in the formation nodded. “At that moment,” CPT Kestner “knew the 112th Signal Battalion was a brotherhood.”

I have known Major General (MG) Dave Bryan since 1985. We were both at a meeting in St Louis to discuss the soon to be fielded Improved High Frequency Radio System (IHFRS). He was there as the Assistant Chief of Staff, Communication-Electronics for the 1st Special Operations Command and I was there from E Co/51st Infantry Regiment as the first Long Range Surveillance Communications Platoon Leader since Vietnam. While

IHFRS was the agenda item, it was clear Major (P) Bryan had bigger ideas - he was on a mission to persuade everyone at the conference and beyond that the Army needed to immediately modernize communications for Special Operations Forces (SOF) – from Special Forces to Training & Administration - all of it with the centerpiece being the creation of a Special Operations Signal Battalion that we know now as the 112th Signal Battalion (Special Operations) (Airborne). As I grew to know him, and work with him, I realized that the creation of the 112th might have been his first, but was not his last opportunity to create something new out of whole cloth. As he did with the 112th – applying his knowledge, experience, and passion for improving

C4 capabilities for SOF and the Signal Regiment – it was his vision of the future that drove him to become Cyberman and establish a baseline for Army Cyber Operations that continues to this day. When I look at his nearly 35 years of military service, I see consistent themes of taking care of people and most importantly, leading by example.

Born and raised in Alabama, General Bryan graduated from Jacksonville State University in 1970 with a plan to teach high school chemistry, math, biology and his true love, literature. Fortunately, as an Army ROTC Scholarship recipient, he owed the Army four years of military service. He never left.

After a year of training where he was taught the basics of Army communications, became qualified as a Parachutist and Pathfinder, and earned his Ranger tab, Bryan was off to Fort Bragg to be the Battalion Signal Officer for 3/325th Airborne Infantry in the 82nd Airborne Division. As a Lieutenant he followed the advice of his father, a retired Master Sergeant, “Listen to your NCOs. Seek their opinion and trust them, but as an officer, always be their leader.” A year later he volunteered for Special Forces training earning his long tab and

Green Beret followed by assignment to a special mission unit at the JFK Center for Military Assistance. An overseas tour in Korea as the S-3 of the 122nd Signal Battalion checked an Army box before he returned to Ft. Bragg for assignment to the Support Battalion, 7th Special Forces Group as the S-4, then as the S-3, and finally as the Commander of the Signal Company. History is vague about whether he stayed in the battalion because was a good communicator or because he was the quarterback of their championship football team, but that not withstanding, Bryan constantly worked above his pay grade to improve SOF communications.

After the Advanced Course, Bryan again returned to the conventional Army with the 3rd Brigade, 1st Armored Division where he encountered a maintenance nightmare with radioteletype (RATT) systems. Leveraging his S-3 experience, Bryan got them fixed and kept them fixed, training the operators and maintenance soldiers with a “field SOP” that he wrote. After three years in Germany, Bryan was assigned to lead training for the US Army Recruiting Command under (then) MG Max Thurman. Thurman appreciated what he saw in Bryan’s complete overhaul of recruiter training and in fact, was the officer who promoted Bryan to Major. Bryan was the author of the Army College Fund program to give Army recruiters a leg up on the other military service recruiters and as an additional duty, Thurman added Bryan to the team that developed the historic “Be All That You Can Be” campaign.

After attending Command and General Staff College, Bryan returned to Fort Bragg in 1984 as the ACofS, C-E of the new 1st Special Operations Command. Coinciding with his return was the drafting of multicommand required operational capability (MROC) 2-84 which was designed to modernize Special Operations Forces. Bryan and his staff inked thirty-six initiatives into MROC 2-84 including everything from rucksack satellite radios, multi-band radios, ruggedized laptops, rechargeable batteries, and yes, a special operations signal unit. Some wanted to call it a “squadron,” others a “communications support element” but Bryan successfully argued that it needed to be a Signal Battalion within the Signal Regiment with companies, platoons, and teams that would serve as the professional hub for special operations communications and communicators. When the Army Vice Chief of Staff (now, GEN Max Thurman) approved the activation of the SOF Signal Battalion, he knew that Bryan had been an early select to LTC and Battalion Command. As part of the approval, he reportedly said, “Activate this Battalion as soon as possible with Bryan in command. He created it, and he’ll get it done.” On September 17, 1986, the 112th Signal Battalion (Special Operations) (Airborne) was activated and LTC Bryan was its first commander, the first “Shadow 6” and in 2013 the 112th’s new compound at Fort Bragg was dedicated in his name.

After command with many operational lessons under his belt, Bryan attended the Army War College where he started looking at the next ridgeline and he wrote his award winning thesis on, “The Future of SOF Communications.” Given his ability to define requirements and drive needed change to include approval and activation of the 112th Signal Battalion (SO), some including myself, refer to Bryan as the “Father of Modern SOF Communications.”

As a LTC(P), Bryan was assigned to the Joint Staff J-6 in 1989 where he became the “go-to” guy during Desert Shield and Desert Storm. In a lessons learned report, he catalogued over 700 interoperability issues between generations of Army, sister service, and Coalition C4 systems which ultimately resulted in the Bryan led “C4I for the Warfighter” program that established the firstever framework for platform-centric, network-centric and decision/knowledge-centric interoperability requirements and programs.

When Bryan assumed command of the 35th Airborne Signal Brigade in 1993, the unit had just completed fielding of the Army MSE systems. He knew that operational deployments were in their near future and that they had to be ready to participate in joint and coalition configurations. Over the next two years they deployed signal support seven times, each time providing first-ever MSE support to joint and coalition forces – in places like Somalia, Haiti, West and Central Africa, to name a few.

As Bryan was being selected for Brigadier General he started his transition from SOF Communicator to Cyber Operator. As the J-6 of the United States Pacific Command (USPACOM), Bryan realized that the Chief Information Officer (CIO) role was no longer a “Garrison” activity, but applied across the force so in 1998 he became the first Combatant Command CIO. Additionally, he recognized the requirement for coalition interoperability and established the Asia-Pacific Area Network (APAN) which is still in use today. He established the first-ever Theater C4 Coordination Center which was the predecessor of today’s Network Operations and Security Centers.

Selected for a second star, Bryan received a dual hat assignment in 2000 as the Vice Director, Defense Information Systems Agency (DISA) and as the Commander of Joint Task Force – Computer Network Defense. As Vice Director, he led the development of a first comprehensive doctrine for network operations and security based on his experiences with the Theater C4 Coordination Center at USPACOM. As JTF Commander he led it through its evolution from JTF-CND to JTF- Computer Network Operations (JTF-CNO) and was the first Title 10 commander to have both the defensive and offensive cyber missions. The command eventually evolved to JTF-Global Network Operations (JTF-GNO). It was during his time as JTF commander that he created the character “Cyberman” and famously appeared in full costume at numerous conferences to bring much needed attention to the need to mainstream and professionalize cyber operations including establishing a cyber career field in the Army and other military services. In the Signal Regiment Museum, his “Cyberman” costume is on display and he is referred to as the “Father of Army Cyber.”

In 2003, Bryan had one last assignment. The Army Chief of Staff, GEN Schoomaker asked him to reorganize and re-equip the Signal Regiment to support the Army’s new modular Brigade concept. The Chief’s guidance was clear, “Give me a lot of the 112th ” in terms of capabilities. Bryan formed and led a team, many members of which had worked for him over the years, and in four months they had a strategy that is still guiding the Regiment today.

Few officers leave a true lasting legacy, but in the case of MG Dave Bryan, he left two. The activation of the 112th Signal Battalion created a touchstone for Army SOF communicators, and the formation of the JTF-CND laid the foundation for today’s CNO. Both were possible because Dave Bryan knew what right looked like and wouldn’t take no for an answer. Always a leader, he continues to be a friend and mentor to many.

Lots of myths abound about MACV -SOG, but two statistics tell the story. Thirteen members of MACV -SOG earned the Medal of Honor and literally 99% of unit members were wounded or killed. Fortunately, Master Sergeant Pelaez was only one of the wounded earning his Purple Heart in 1969 while providing covering fire to allow his Team to break contact. He recovered and then returned to Vietnam and MACV -SOG to teach in the Reconnaissance Team Leaders Course and then serve as an adviser to a Cambodian Battalion before returning to the United States. After serving with 7th SFG (A) in Panama, Master Sergeant Pelaez again volunteered, this time for JSOC where he was the first Plank Owner for the newly established Joint Communications Unit (JCU). With JCU he continued to support real world operations including deploying with SFOD -Delta for the BG Dozier rescue in Italy and culminating with a parachute jump into Grenada as part of Urgent Fury. After service as a First Sergeant at the Joint Communications Support Element (JCSE) and attendance at the Sergeant’s Major Academy, Master Sergeant Pelaez retired from active duty in

1989. Similar to Mr. Zeisman, after retiring from uniform, Master Sergeant Pelaez became a government civilian at USSOCOM continuing to support the Special Operations Force, starting out with C4 requirements and culminating as the J6 Deputy for Operations where he was the absolute continuity as USSOCOM reorganized for the 21st century. As the Crusty Uncle, Mike Pelaez provided mentorship and guidance across the Force and to numerous J6s and CIOs. The Special Operations community treats communications as the mission critical task that it is and develops communicators to meet that task. Mr. Zeisman and Mr. Pelaez are two of many who have risen to the challenge and been Special Operations Communicators.
